# Cleaning — what your report's recommended treatment means
A professional cleaning — called *scaling and polishing* in clinical terms, often just *scaling* in everyday conversation — is the removal of plaque and calculus (hardened plaque, also called tartar) from the surfaces of your teeth using specialised dental instruments. It is the most common dental procedure and the foundation of preventive dental care.
If your report's Potential Actions table recommends a cleaning, it almost always sits at Low priority — even on a High overall risk report. That is not because the cleaning is unimportant; it's because cleaning is part of long-term oral health management rather than urgent intervention. Most people benefit from a professional cleaning every six to twelve months; people with higher plaque accumulation, gum disease, or other risk factors may benefit from more frequent visits.


## Why home brushing is not enough
No matter how thoroughly you brush, plaque begins to harden into calculus within about 24 to 72 hours. Once it has hardened, calculus cannot be removed by brushing or flossing — only the dentist's instruments can dislodge it. Calculus accumulates in places that are hard to clean even with perfect technique: behind the lower front teeth, between teeth, and along the gum line on the cheek-side of back teeth.
Calculus matters because it provides a rough surface that more plaque sticks to, and because it sits right at the gum line where it irritates the gum tissue. The longer calculus is left, the more likely gum inflammation (gingivitis) and eventually gum disease (periodontitis) become. A professional cleaning at regular intervals is the most reliable way to prevent this cycle.


## What is involved
A standard professional cleaning typically takes about 30 to 45 minutes and includes:

1. **Examination.** The dentist or dental hygienist looks for any obvious problems, gum inflammation, and areas that may need closer attention.
2. **Scaling.** Calculus and heavy plaque are removed from the tooth surfaces, both above and just at the gum line. This is usually done with an ultrasonic scaler — a small instrument that uses high-frequency vibration and a water spray. Hand instruments may be used for finer work.
3. **Polishing.** A polishing paste is used with a small rotating brush or rubber cup to smooth the tooth surfaces and remove surface stains. This makes the teeth feel smoother and slightly whiter.
4. **Fluoride application** (in some clinics, particularly for higher-risk patients).
5. **Advice on home care.** The dentist or hygienist may show you which areas you tend to miss when brushing, and recommend changes to technique, toothbrush type, or interdental cleaning.

If your gums are inflamed or bleeding, the cleaning may feel slightly uncomfortable, but it should not be painful. Clinics can offer topical numbing gel for sensitive patients. After the cleaning, the gums often look better within a few days, and any bleeding tends to reduce as the inflammation settles.



## How a cleaning differs from "deep cleaning"
A standard professional cleaning addresses plaque and calculus *above and at* the gum line. A more involved procedure called *scaling and root planing* (sometimes called a "deep cleaning") goes below the gum line to clean root surfaces in pockets that have formed due to gum disease. Deep cleaning is usually reserved for more advanced cases — Moderate or Advanced plaque with significant gum disease — and is sometimes performed across two or more visits, often with local anaesthetic.
If your report flags Advanced plaque or significant gum recession, the dentist may recommend scaling and root planing rather than a standard cleaning. The recommendation in the Potential Actions table is a starting point — the in-person examination determines what is actually needed.



## Cost and subsidy
A standard professional cleaning is typically the most affordable dental procedure. Restructured hospitals, polyclinics, and private clinics in Singapore each have different pricing. Medisave does not typically cover routine cleaning; CHAS subsidies often apply for eligible card holders at participating clinics. ## How often should I have a cleaning?
The general guideline is every six to twelve months, with more frequent cleanings for people who have:
- A history of gum disease
- Heavy plaque or calculus build-up between visits
- Diabetes or other conditions that affect gum health
- A habit of smoking
- Difficulty cleaning effectively at home (for example, due to crowded teeth or restorations)
Your dentist will recommend an interval based on what they see at the visit.

# Crown — what your report's recommended treatment means
A crown (sometimes called a "cap") is a custom-made covering that fits over the entire visible portion of a tooth. It restores the tooth's shape, strength, and appearance when the tooth has been damaged or weakened to the point that a standard filling is no longer sufficient on its own.
If your screening report's Potential Actions table recommends a crown, it usually means one of the following is true on that tooth: the existing decay or damage is too extensive for a filling alone to restore reliably; a previous large filling has fractured or compromised the surrounding tooth structure; or the tooth has had a root canal and now needs a crown to protect what remains of it.



## Why a crown rather than a filling
The simplest way to understand the difference: a filling replaces a portion of the tooth, while a crown replaces the *outside* of the tooth. As decay or damage gets larger, more and more of the tooth structure is lost. At some point what's left is not strong enough to hold a filling under chewing forces — the filling would simply fail. A crown solves this by surrounding the remaining tooth on all sides and giving it back its full shape and biting strength.
A common rule of thumb: if more than about half the tooth's natural structure is missing, or if the tooth has had a root canal, a crown is the more durable choice.



## What a crown is made of
Modern crowns are typically made of one of:
- **Porcelain (or ceramic).** Tooth-coloured, used most often for front teeth and increasingly for back teeth. Looks the most natural.
- **Porcelain fused to metal.** A metal core gives strength, with porcelain on the outside for appearance. Older but still common.
- **Full metal (gold or other alloy).** Strong and long-lasting, but visible. More common on back teeth where appearance matters less.
- **Zirconia.** Very strong, tooth-coloured, increasingly common in modern dentistry.

Your dentist will recommend a material based on the tooth's location, the bite forces involved, and your preferences.



## What to expect at the visits
A traditional crown usually takes two appointments:
1. **First visit (preparation).** The dentist removes any decay and shapes the remaining tooth so it can accept the crown. An impression or digital scan is taken. A temporary crown is placed while the permanent crown is made (usually at a dental laboratory, taking one to two weeks).
2. **Second visit (fitting).** The temporary is removed and the permanent crown is checked, adjusted for fit and bite, and cemented in place.
Some clinics in Singapore offer same-day crowns using digital scanning and in-office milling — the whole process is completed in one visit.
You should not feel pain during either visit; local anaesthetic is used during the preparation. Some sensitivity in the days afterwards is normal and usually settles.



## How long crowns last
A well-made crown, on a tooth that is otherwise healthy and is well cared for at home, typically lasts ten to fifteen years or longer. The crown itself does not decay, but the natural tooth underneath can — so brushing twice a day, daily interdental cleaning, and regular dental check-ups remain important.



## Cost and subsidy
Crowns are a moderately costly procedure. In Singapore, costs vary substantially between care settings — restructured hospitals (such as the National Dental Centre Singapore), polyclinics, and private dental clinics each have different fee structures. Medisave can typically be used for the surgical or restorative portion of a crown procedure subject to scheme limits, and CHAS subsidies may apply for eligible card holders depending on the care setting. # Extraction — what your report's recommended treatment means
An extraction is the removal of a tooth that cannot be saved or that is causing problems significant enough that removal is the appropriate option. Modern dentistry strongly favours saving teeth wherever possible — through fillings, crowns, root canals, or periodontal treatment — but in some situations extraction is the right choice.



## When extraction is recommended
Common reasons a dentist may recommend extraction include:
- **A tooth too damaged to restore.** If the decay or fracture has destroyed too much of the tooth to support a filling or crown reliably, extraction may be the more practical option than an extensive (and ultimately unsuccessful) restoration attempt.
- **Severe gum disease.** If periodontal disease has caused too much bone loss around a tooth, the tooth may be too loose to keep, and extraction prevents the disease spreading to neighbouring teeth.
- **Failed root canal that cannot be retreated.** Where a root canal has not resolved an infection and retreatment or apicoectomy is not feasible.
- **Impacted or problematic wisdom teeth.** Wisdom teeth that are partially erupted, repeatedly inflamed, decayed, or pushing on neighbouring teeth are often best removed.
- **Crowding for orthodontic reasons.** Sometimes healthy teeth are removed to make space for the remaining teeth to align properly during orthodontic treatment.
- **A tooth that is already symptomatic and not restorable.** Persistent pain, abscess, or infection in a tooth where treatment options have run out.



## Types of extraction
Extractions fall into two broad categories:
- **Simple extraction.** Performed when the tooth is fully erupted and can be removed with forceps and gentle pressure. Usually done by a general dentist, under local anaesthetic, and typically takes 15 to 30 minutes.
- **Surgical extraction.** Required when the tooth is broken below the gum line, has curved or fragile roots, or is impacted (partially or fully under the gum, as is common with wisdom teeth). Involves making a small incision in the gum, sometimes removing a small amount of bone, and removing the tooth in pieces if necessary. May be performed by a general dentist or referred to an oral surgeon.
Both procedures are done under local anaesthetic. Sedation or general anaesthetic is sometimes offered for surgical extractions, particularly impacted wisdom teeth or for patients with significant dental anxiety.



## What to expect during the procedure
The basic sequence is:
1. **Local anaesthetic.** The area around the tooth is numbed thoroughly. You should feel pressure but not pain.
2. **Loosening and removal.** The dentist uses a small instrument called an *elevator* to loosen the tooth from the surrounding ligament, then forceps to remove it. For surgical extractions, additional steps (incision, removing bone, sectioning the tooth) are taken as needed.
3. **Wound care.** The socket is cleaned, sometimes a few stitches are placed, and a piece of gauze is bitten on to control bleeding.
The procedure itself typically takes 15 to 45 minutes for a single tooth, longer for surgical cases or multiple teeth.



## After the procedure
A normal recovery follows a predictable pattern:
 **First 24 hours.** Some bleeding, mild to moderate discomfort, and possible swelling. Bite on the gauze for the first 30 to 45 minutes; replace if needed. Cold compresses on the cheek help reduce swelling. Eat soft foods and avoid hot drinks.
- **First few days.** Discomfort gradually decreases. Avoid rinsing forcefully, smoking, drinking through a straw, or vigorous exercise — these can dislodge the blood clot and lead to *dry socket*, a painful complication.
- **First two weeks.** The gum tissue heals over the socket. Stitches, if used, may dissolve on their own or be removed at a follow-up visit.
- **First few months.** The bone gradually fills in the socket beneath the gum. Most people are fully healed within three months.

Over-the-counter pain relief is usually sufficient; the dentist will prescribe stronger medication or antibiotics if indicated.



## Replacement considerations
Once a tooth is extracted, it is worth thinking about replacement — particularly for teeth other than wisdom teeth. Without replacement, the consequences of a missing tooth (drift of adjacent teeth, supereruption of the opposing tooth, bone resorption, bite changes) gradually develop over months and years. Replacement options include:
- **Implant** — typically the best long-term option, but needs sufficient bone and is the most expensive. Often planned to be placed three to six months after extraction to allow healing.
- **Bridge** — fixed replacement using adjacent teeth as anchors.
- **Partial denture** — removable, more affordable, suitable for multiple missing teeth.
- **No replacement** — sometimes appropriate, particularly for wisdom teeth or in specific orthodontic situations.
Discussing replacement *before* the extraction, where possible, is useful because some options (particularly implants) benefit from planning that starts before the tooth is removed.



## Cost and subsidy
Extractions vary widely in cost depending on whether they are simple or surgical, the tooth involved, and the care setting. Wisdom tooth surgical extractions are generally the most expensive single extractions. Medisave can often be used for surgical extractions within scheme limits (simple extractions usually cannot be claimed); CHAS subsidies apply for eligible card holders at participating clinics. # Filling — what your report's recommended treatment means
A filling is a procedure to repair a tooth that has been damaged by decay or, less commonly, by minor fracture or wear. The dentist removes the decayed or damaged portion of the tooth and fills the resulting hole with a restorative material that returns the tooth to a functional, cleanable shape.
A filling is the most common dental treatment for a cavity, and it is the appropriate choice when:
- The decay has not reached the nerve at the centre of the tooth (the *pulp*)
- Enough sound tooth structure remains around the cavity to support a filling
- The cavity is small to moderate in size
If the cavity is too deep, has reached the nerve, or has destroyed too much of the tooth, a more involved treatment — a crown, a root canal, or in some cases an extraction — is needed instead.



## What fillings are made of
Two materials are used most commonly today:
- **Composite resin (white filling, tooth-coloured filling).** A blend of plastic and fine glass particles. Bonded directly to the tooth, shaped, and hardened with a curing light. Tooth-coloured and aesthetic, suitable for any tooth, and the standard choice in most modern dental practice.
- **Glass ionomer.** A material that releases small amounts of fluoride over time. Used in specific situations, particularly in children and in cavities below the gum line.
Older fillings were often made of *amalgam* (a silver-coloured alloy of mercury, silver, tin, and copper). Amalgam is still used in some settings — it is durable and well-tested — but composite resin has become the default for new fillings in Singapore and most of the world.



## What to expect at the visit
A filling appointment typically takes 30 to 60 minutes for a single tooth:
1. **Local anaesthetic.** A small injection numbs the area around the tooth. You should not feel pain during the procedure, though you may feel pressure or vibration.
2. **Removal of decay.** The dentist uses a small drill and other instruments to remove the decayed portion of the tooth. Only the affected tissue is removed; healthy tooth is preserved.
3. **Cleaning and preparation.** The cavity is cleaned and shaped to receive the filling.
4. **Placement of the filling.** Composite resin is placed in layers, each hardened with a curing light. The filling is shaped to match the natural contour of the tooth.
5. **Bite check and polish.** The dentist checks your bite, makes any adjustments, and polishes the filling so it feels smooth.
Most people leave the appointment able to eat normally once the anaesthetic wears off (usually one to three hours later). Some sensitivity to cold or pressure is normal in the days afterwards and usually settles within a week or two.



## How long fillings last
A well-placed composite filling on a tooth that is otherwise healthy typically lasts five to ten years, often longer. Fillings can fail over time — they can chip, leak around the edges, or wear down — and may need replacement. Larger fillings tend to last less well than smaller ones, which is part of why an early-stage cavity treated promptly often gives a better long-term outcome than a larger one treated later.



## When a filling is not enough
If, when the dentist removes the decay, it turns out the cavity extends close to or into the nerve, the recommended treatment may shift mid-procedure to a root canal followed by a crown. Similarly, if too little tooth structure remains to support a filling reliably, a crown may be recommended instead. Your dentist will discuss this with you before proceeding.



## Cost and subsidy
Fillings are among the more affordable restorative procedures. Costs in Singapore vary by tooth (front teeth and small fillings are typically less expensive than large fillings on back teeth) and by care setting. Medisave generally does not cover fillings; CHAS subsidies often apply for eligible card holders at participating clinics. # Implant — what your report's recommended treatment means
A dental implant is a fixed (non-removable) replacement for a missing tooth. It consists of three parts: a titanium post that is placed into the jawbone where the tooth root used to be, an *abutment* that connects to the post above the gum line, and a *crown* that sits on top and looks and functions like a natural tooth.
If your report's Potential Actions table recommends an implant, it usually reflects that you have one or more missing teeth where a fixed, long-lasting replacement is appropriate — and where the surrounding bone and gum tissue are sufficient to support an implant. Implants are typically the most durable and most natural-feeling tooth replacement option, but they are also the most involved and most expensive.


## When an implant is the right choice
Implants suit a range of situations, particularly:
- **A single missing tooth** where the adjacent teeth are healthy and you want to avoid preparing them for a bridge.
- **Multiple missing teeth** that can be supported by a small number of implants (an implant-supported bridge or denture).
- **A long-term solution.** Implants, well placed in healthy bone and well cared for, often last 20 years or more.
- **Better preservation of the jawbone.** Because the implant is placed into the bone, it stimulates the bone the way a natural tooth root does — reducing the bone loss that typically follows tooth loss.
Implants may not be suitable for everyone. Factors that the dentist will assess include the amount and quality of jawbone (a tooth missing for many years may have caused bone resorption that requires bone grafting first), the health of the gums, smoking status (smoking significantly reduces implant success rates), and certain medical conditions such as poorly controlled diabetes or some bone medications.


## What to expect
A standard implant procedure typically takes several months from start to finish, divided into two or three main stages:
1. **Assessment and planning.** The dentist examines your mouth, takes X-rays and often a 3D scan (CBCT) to assess bone volume and plan the implant position. If there is insufficient bone, a bone graft may be discussed at this stage.
2. **Implant placement (surgery 1).** The titanium post is placed into the jawbone under local anaesthetic, sometimes with sedation. The procedure typically takes 60 to 90 minutes per implant. Some clinics use guided surgery (computer-planned) for greater precision.
3. **Healing (osseointegration).** The bone gradually fuses with the titanium post over three to six months. During this time you may have a temporary tooth or denture for appearance.
4. **Abutment placement (surgery 2, sometimes done at the same time as stage 2).** Once healing is complete, the abutment is attached to the post.
5. **Crown placement.** Impressions are taken (or digital scans) and the final crown is fabricated and fitted onto the abutment.
The whole process from initial consultation to final crown typically takes four to nine months for a straightforward case, longer if a bone graft is needed.


## What it's like to live with an implant
Once healed, an implant feels and functions very much like a natural tooth:
- **Eating** is unrestricted. You can chew normally on the implant.
- **Speaking** is unaffected.
- **Cleaning** is similar to a natural tooth — brushing twice a day and daily interdental cleaning. Special care around the gum margin of the implant is important to prevent *peri-implantitis*, an inflammatory condition that can develop around implants if plaque is not controlled.
- **Appearance** is typically very good — a well-made implant crown is hard to distinguish from natural teeth.


## How long implants last
The implant post itself, in healthy bone with good home care and regular dental visits, typically lasts decades — often a lifetime. The crown on top has a shorter expected lifespan (10–15 years is common) and may eventually need to be replaced even when the implant post itself is still sound.


## Cost and subsidy
Implants are the most expensive of the common tooth replacement options. Costs in Singapore vary substantially by care setting and by the complexity of the case (single implant in good bone, vs. multiple implants requiring bone grafting). Medisave can often be used for the surgical portion of an implant procedure within scheme limits; CHAS subsidies generally apply only to a limited extent for implants. # Partial denture — what your report's recommended treatment means
A partial denture is a removable appliance that replaces one or more missing teeth. It consists of artificial teeth attached to a base — usually pink to match the gum colour — that rests against your remaining teeth and gums. You can take it out for cleaning and put it back in for normal daily use.
If your report's Potential Actions table recommends a partial denture, it usually reflects that you have one or more missing teeth and that a removable appliance is one of the most accessible ways to restore chewing function and appearance. Partial dentures are a common starting point especially when several teeth are missing, when budget is a consideration, or when other replacement options (bridge, implant) are not suitable for that particular case.



## Why a partial denture might be recommended
Partial dentures suit a range of situations:
- **Multiple missing teeth in a row, or in different parts of the mouth.** A single partial denture can replace several missing teeth at once.
- **Insufficient bone for an implant** — for example, where a tooth has been missing for many years and the supporting bone has resorbed.
- **Adjacent teeth that should not be used as anchors for a bridge.** A bridge requires the dentist to prepare the teeth on either side of the gap; if those teeth are healthy, a partial denture avoids that.
- **Budget considerations.** Partial dentures are typically the most affordable replacement option.
- **A staged approach.** A partial denture can be a useful interim solution while you plan and save for an implant or bridge.



## What partial dentures are made of
There are two common types:
- **Acrylic partial dentures.** The base is pink acrylic plastic, often with metal clasps that hold the denture in place by gripping adjacent teeth. Lighter and less expensive; suitable for many cases.
- **Cobalt-chrome (metal-framework) partial dentures.** A metal framework supports the acrylic and artificial teeth. Stronger, thinner, and usually fits more precisely. More expensive but generally preferred for long-term use.
A newer option, the *flexible partial denture* (made of a flexible nylon-based material), is sometimes used for small partials in specific situations, particularly where appearance matters and metal clasps would be visible.



## What to expect at the visits
Making a partial denture typically involves four to five appointments over a few weeks:
1. **Initial assessment.** The dentist examines your mouth, takes X-rays if needed, and discusses the design of the denture.
2. **Impressions.** Detailed moulds of your teeth and gums are taken (or digital scans, depending on the clinic). For a metal-framework denture, a more detailed second impression may be taken at a later visit.
3. **Try-in.** A wax version of the denture is tried in your mouth so the fit, bite, and tooth position can be checked and adjusted before the final denture is made.
4. **Fitting.** The finished denture is fitted, adjusted for comfort, and you are shown how to insert, remove, and clean it.
5. **Review.** A follow-up visit a week or two later checks how you are getting on, and any sore spots are adjusted.



## What it's like to live with a partial denture
Most people find a partial denture takes a couple of weeks to feel normal:
- **Eating** can feel different at first. Starting with softer foods, cutting food into smaller pieces, and chewing on both sides of the mouth helps. Most people return to a normal diet within a few weeks.
- **Speaking** may feel slightly different initially, particularly with sounds that involve the tongue near the front teeth. Reading aloud at home for the first few days speeds up adjustment.
- **Cleaning** the denture is essential — it should be removed and brushed daily (a separate denture brush is recommended), soaked in a denture cleaner, and the mouth and remaining teeth should be cleaned as normal.
- **Wearing it overnight** is generally not recommended — taking the denture out at night gives the gum tissue time to rest and reduces the risk of infections.
- **Sore spots** are common in the first week or two and are easily adjusted by the dentist; let them know rather than putting up with discomfort.



## How long partial dentures last
A well-made partial denture typically lasts five to ten years, sometimes longer with good care. The fit may change over time as your mouth changes — gums and bone change shape gradually, and remaining teeth may shift slightly. Periodic relines (where the inside surface of the denture is rebuilt) can extend its life.



## Cost and subsidy
Partial dentures are typically the most affordable tooth replacement option. Costs in Singapore vary by care setting and by the type of denture (acrylic vs cobalt-chrome). Medisave can often be used for tooth replacement procedures within scheme limits; CHAS subsidies apply at participating clinics for eligible card holders. # Root canal — what your report's recommended treatment means
Root canal treatment (sometimes called *endodontic treatment*) is a procedure to save a tooth where the decay or infection has reached the nerve at the centre of the tooth. The dentist removes the infected or inflamed nerve tissue, cleans and disinfects the inside of the tooth, fills the empty space with a sealing material, and seals the tooth so it can continue to function.
If a root canal has been recommended, it usually means one of the following: an Advanced Cavity has progressed deep enough to involve the nerve; an existing large filling has failed and bacteria have reached the pulp; the tooth has been damaged by trauma; or the tooth has shown signs of pulp inflammation or infection (such as a persistent ache, sensitivity to heat, or an abscess at the gum).
The goal of a root canal is straightforward: to keep the natural tooth in place rather than extracting it. A natural tooth, even after a root canal, generally provides better long-term function than any replacement.



## Why root canals have an undeserved reputation
Many people associate root canals with pain. The reality of modern endodontic treatment is the opposite: the tooth is usually painful *before* the root canal because the nerve is inflamed or infected, and the procedure relieves that pain by removing the source. With effective local anaesthetic and modern instruments, the procedure itself feels comparable to a large filling — pressure and vibration, but not pain. Most patients are surprised by how routine it is.



## What to expect at the visits
A root canal is typically completed in one to three appointments depending on the tooth (front teeth are usually quicker; molars with multiple roots take longer):
1. **Local anaesthetic.** The tooth is numbed thoroughly. You should feel no pain during the procedure.
2. **Isolation.** A small rubber sheet (a *rubber dam*) is placed around the tooth to keep it dry and prevent bacteria from saliva entering the tooth during treatment.
3. **Access.** A small opening is made through the top of the tooth to reach the nerve canals.
4. **Cleaning.** The infected or inflamed nerve tissue is removed using fine instruments. The canals are cleaned, shaped, and disinfected.
5. **Filling.** The cleaned canals are filled with a rubber-like material called *gutta-percha* and sealed.
6. **Temporary or permanent restoration.** A temporary filling is placed at the access opening; usually the tooth is then prepared for a crown at a follow-up visit, because a tooth that has had a root canal is more brittle and benefits from the protection a crown provides.
A modern root canal on a front tooth typically takes 60 to 90 minutes; a molar can take 90 to 120 minutes per visit, sometimes spread across two visits.



## After the procedure
Most people return to normal eating once the anaesthetic wears off, though it is wise to chew gently on that side until the permanent crown is placed. Some tenderness when biting is normal for a few days and usually settles. Over-the-counter pain relief is typically sufficient.
A root canal is generally followed within a few weeks by a crown, particularly on back teeth. Without a crown, the treated tooth is more likely to fracture under chewing forces. Front teeth occasionally do not need a full crown; the dentist will advise.



## Success and longevity
Root canal treatment has a high success rate — typically over 90% for straightforward cases when followed by an appropriate restoration. A tooth that has had a root canal and a crown can last decades. As with all dental work, the tooth itself can still develop further decay around the edges of the restoration, so good home care and regular check-ups remain important.
In a small percentage of cases the original treatment fails — the infection persists or recurs — and the tooth may need *retreatment* (a second root canal) or, less commonly, a small surgical procedure called an *apicoectomy*. If retreatment is not feasible or successful, extraction may eventually be necessary.



## When extraction might be the better choice
A root canal is usually preferred over extraction because keeping the natural tooth is preferable. However, in some cases — for example, where the tooth is too damaged to restore reliably, where the root anatomy is unfavourable, or where the long-term outlook is poor — extraction followed by a replacement (implant, bridge, or partial denture) may be the more practical option. Your dentist will discuss the trade-offs.


## Cost and subsidy
Root canal treatment is moderately costly, particularly on molars where multiple root canals are involved. Costs in Singapore vary by tooth (single-canal front teeth are less expensive than multi-canal molars) and by care setting. Medisave can often be used for endodontic procedures within scheme limits; CHAS subsidies apply for eligible card holders at participating clinics. A subsequent crown is typically additional.
