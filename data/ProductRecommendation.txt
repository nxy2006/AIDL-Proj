# Dental floss
Dental floss is a thin nylon (or PTFE) thread used to remove plaque and food debris from between teeth — surfaces that a toothbrush cannot reach. It remains the most accessible interdental cleaning tool, and for users with tightly-spaced teeth and healthy gums it is often the right choice.
For users whose report flagged plaque on multiple teeth, daily flossing is one of the higher-leverage changes available — but only if it is done daily and with reasonable technique. Sporadic flossing yields little benefit; consistent flossing yields significant benefit.


## Who this category suits
Dental floss suits:
- **Users with tightly-spaced teeth** where interdental brushes do not fit comfortably between teeth.
- **Users with healthy gums** and no major restorations (crowns, bridges, implants) — interdental brushes or water flossers are often more practical for those situations.
- **Users who already have an established daily flossing habit** that is working — there's no compelling reason to switch tools if it's effective.
- **Users who travel often** — floss is portable and disposable.
- **Users on a budget** — floss is the most affordable interdental option.
For users with wider gaps between teeth, gum recession, crowns, bridges, or braces, an interdental brush or water flosser is usually more effective. See `product-interdental-brushes` and `product-water-flosser`.


## Types of floss
- **Waxed floss** — coated in wax to slide more easily between tightly-spaced teeth. The default for most adults.
- **Unwaxed floss** — slightly thinner; some users prefer the texture. Shreds more easily on rough fillings or crowns.
- **PTFE (Teflon-based) floss** — slides smoothly even between very tight contacts; less likely to shred. Often more expensive.
- **Dental tape** — a wider, ribbon-like floss that can feel gentler on the gums; suitable for slightly wider gaps.
- **Floss picks (disposable Y- or F-shaped handles with a short floss segment)** — easier to handle than spool floss, useful for users with limited dexterity. Less effective than spool floss for getting around the C-shape of each tooth, but better than not flossing at all.
- **Super floss / orthodontic floss** — has a stiff threader at one end and a spongy section, designed for braces, bridges, and around implants.
For most users, waxed standard floss is fine. Picks are a reasonable compromise for users who genuinely will not use spool floss.


## How to use
The technique that gets the most plaque off the tooth surface:
1. **Take 30–45 cm of floss.** Wrap most of it around the middle finger of one hand and a small amount around the middle finger of the other.
2. **Hold a 2–3 cm working section** between thumbs and index fingers.
3. **Slide the floss gently between two teeth** using a side-to-side sawing motion — don't snap it down.
4. **Curve the floss into a C-shape against the side of one tooth**, then slide it up and down the tooth, gently going slightly under the gum line. Repeat against the side of the neighbouring tooth.
5. **Use a fresh section of floss for each gap** to avoid transferring bacteria.
6. **Cover every gap** including the back side of the back teeth.
The full mouth takes about 60–90 seconds with practice. Initial sessions are slower; the time drops as the technique becomes habit.



## Common mistakes
- Snapping the floss down between teeth, which can cut the gum
- Using one short section of floss for the whole mouth (transfers bacteria from one gap to another)
- Sawing back and forth at the gum line rather than wrapping around each tooth
- Skipping the back teeth ("can't reach")
- Stopping after a few days because the gums bleed — bleeding is from inflammation, not from injury, and consistent daily flossing is what makes it stop


## When to switch tools
If, after two to three weeks of consistent daily flossing with reasonable technique, you find that:
- Floss shreds repeatedly on the same tooth — there may be a rough filling or crown edge that needs attention from a dentist
- Bleeding doesn't decrease after three weeks — the gum issue may be more than simple gingivitis; see a dentist
- Flossing feels physically difficult or you simply won't sustain it daily — try interdental brushes or a water flosser instead. Daily use of a "less effective" tool consistently outperforms occasional use of a "more effective" tool.

# Interdental brush
Interdental brushes are small wire brushes shaped like miniature bottle brushes, sized to fit between specific pairs of teeth. For many users — particularly those with gum recession, wider spaces between teeth, crowns, bridges, or implants — they remove more plaque per minute than dental floss and are often easier to use consistently.
If your report flagged Moderate or Advanced plaque alongside any gum recession, interdental brushes are very likely the right tool for at least some areas of your mouth.


## Who this category suits
Interdental brushes suit:
- **Users with Moderate or Advanced plaque accumulation**, particularly at the gum line and between teeth.
- **Users with gum recession**, where the gaps between teeth have opened up and floss alone can no longer reach the entire surface.
- **Users with crowns, bridges, or implants**, around which interdental brushes clean more thoroughly than floss.
- **Users with braces or other orthodontic appliances**.
- **Users who find flossing physically awkward** — interdental brushes are often easier on the hands and don't require wrapping or threading.
- **Most adults, frankly** — many dentists in Singapore now suggest interdental brushes as the preferred interdental tool unless tightly-spaced teeth specifically require floss.


## Sizing — the most important detail
Interdental brushes are sized by the diameter of the wire-and-bristle head, typically labelled in millimetres or by an alphabetical/numerical scale. **Most users need two or three different sizes** for different parts of the mouth — gaps between front teeth are typically smaller than gaps between back teeth.
Practical sizing approach:
1. **Buy a variety pack** the first time, with three or four sizes.
2. **Try each size** in different gaps. The right brush should fit *snugly* — going in with gentle resistance, sweeping the sides of both adjacent teeth, but not requiring force.
3. **Force is the warning sign.** If a brush requires real pressure to insert, it's too big — either move down a size, or that gap may simply suit floss better.
4. **Note which size works where.** Most adults end up with a small size for front teeth and a medium size for back teeth, but this varies.
5. **Re-check sizes if your gums change** — after periodontal treatment, after extractions, or if recession progresses, the gaps and the appropriate sizes may change.

A dentist or dental therapist can point out which sizes suit your mouth at the next visit.



## How to use
1. **Insert gently between two teeth**, angled slightly toward the gum line. Don't push if it doesn't go in — change to a smaller size.
2. **Push through and back out a few times.** The bristles sweep both adjacent tooth surfaces and clean the area between.
3. **No toothpaste is needed**, though some users like to dip the brush in mouthwash.
4. **Rinse the brush** under running water after each use to clear debris.
5. **Replace the brush** when the bristles splay or look matted — usually every one to two weeks for daily use. A worn brush is much less effective.
A full pass through the mouth typically takes 60–90 seconds.



## When floss is better
Despite the advantages of interdental brushes, some situations still favour floss:
- **Very tight contacts between teeth** where even the smallest interdental brush won't fit
- **Users with all-natural teeth, no recession, no restorations** — floss reaches the contact area effectively in this profile
- **Cleaning right under the contact point** between two tightly-spaced teeth — floss does this better
- **Travel** — floss is more portable
In practice, many users benefit from using both: floss in the tightly-spaced front area, interdental brushes in the back where access is better.


## Care of the brush
- Rinse and shake dry after each use
- Store upright in a clean, dry place
- Replace when bristles splay, the wire bends visibly, or every two weeks at the latest
- Don't share interdental brushes (the same applies to toothbrushes and floss)

# Alcohol-free mouthwash
Alcohol-free mouthwash is any mouthwash that omits ethanol from the formulation. The product itself can be a fluoride rinse, an antiseptic rinse, or a combination — what differs is the absence of alcohol as a vehicle. For many users, the alcohol-free version of a mouthwash is the better choice, and it is increasingly the default in modern formulations.


## Who this category suits
Alcohol-free mouthwash is preferable to alcohol-containing alternatives for:
- **Users with gum recession**, where the alcohol can sting exposed root surfaces and contribute to discomfort.
- **Users with dry mouth** from medication, medical conditions, or aging — alcohol is mildly drying and can worsen the underlying problem.
- **Users with mouth ulcers, canker sores, or oral inflammation**, where alcohol is irritating.
- **Users recovering from dental procedures** (extractions, implants, periodontal surgery) where alcohol can interfere with healing.
- **Users in cancer treatment**, where mucosal sensitivity is high and alcohol can cause significant irritation.
- **Children and teenagers**, who may swallow rather than spit reliably (alcohol mouthwashes are not recommended for younger children).
- **Users who avoid alcohol for personal, religious, or recovery reasons** — many alcohol-free products are clearly labelled and available in major Singapore retailers.
For users with no specific reason to prefer alcohol-free, alcohol-containing mouthwashes (where used appropriately) are not problematic and can be slightly more effective at dissolving certain plaque components. For most others, alcohol-free is the better default.



## What to look for
The mouthwash category — fluoride, antiseptic, daily use — matters more than the alcohol question alone. Common alcohol-free pairings:
- **Alcohol-free daily fluoride rinse** — see `product-mouthwash-fluoride`. Most major fluoride mouthwashes have alcohol-free versions; the standard fluoride concentration applies (~225 ppm).
- **Alcohol-free antiseptic rinse** — typically essential-oil or CPC-based. See `product-mouthwash-antiseptic`. Effects on plaque and gingivitis are comparable to alcohol-containing equivalents in most studies.
- **Alcohol-free chlorhexidine** — increasingly available as the default formulation, particularly because chlorhexidine is often prescribed in situations where alcohol would be contraindicated (post-surgery).
Other features to look for:
- **Mild flavour** that does not deter daily use
- **Suitable for daily long-term use** (most alcohol-free fluoride and CPC products) versus short-term courses (chlorhexidine)
- **Compatibility with your dental work** — implants, crowns, bridges all benefit from a non-irritating rinse



## How to use
The use pattern depends on the underlying mouthwash type — fluoride mouthwashes mid-day at a different time from brushing, antiseptic mouthwashes per the product's stated duration, and so on. See the relevant entries:
- `product-mouthwash-fluoride`
- `product-mouthwash-antiseptic`
The alcohol-free choice does not change the timing recommendations, only removes the irritation issues for sensitive users.



## On the alcohol-and-cancer question
A historical concern raised about alcohol-containing mouthwashes was a possible link to oral cancer. The current evidence picture is that:
- Standard daily use of alcohol-containing mouthwash has not been shown to meaningfully increase oral cancer risk in well-designed studies.
- Tobacco use and heavy alcohol consumption from drinking remain the dominant oral cancer risk factors by a wide margin.
- That said, alcohol-free mouthwash avoids the question entirely, and for users with multiple oral cancer risk factors (heavy smoking, regular heavy alcohol consumption) it is a sensible additional precaution.
In practice the decision usually comes down to comfort, dryness, and existing dental work — not cancer risk.

# Antiseptic mouthwash
Antiseptic mouthwashes contain antibacterial ingredients designed to reduce the bacteria in the mouth and the plaque they form. They differ in strength, evidence base, and intended duration of use — so the practical question is usually not "do I need an antiseptic mouthwash" but "which one, for how long, and why."
If your screening report flagged Moderate or Advanced plaque, an antiseptic mouthwash may be useful as a short-term adjunct to good brushing and interdental cleaning, particularly around the time of a professional cleaning or periodontal treatment. The keyword is *adjunct* — none of these products replace the mechanical work of brushing and cleaning between teeth.

## The main categories
### Chlorhexidine (typically 0.12% or 0.2%)
The most clinically potent antiseptic available in mouthwash form. Highly effective at reducing plaque and gum inflammation. Limitations:
- **Short-term use only** — typically two to four weeks at a time, often prescribed after periodontal treatment, around oral surgery, or for short courses to break a plaque cycle. Not appropriate as a daily long-term rinse.
- **Causes brown staining of teeth and tongue with extended use** — the staining is reversible with professional cleaning but is unsightly while it lasts.
- **Alters taste perception** during use, particularly for salty and bitter flavours.
- **Available by prescription** in Singapore at the higher concentrations; some lower-concentration versions are over-the-counter.
- **Don't use within 30 minutes of brushing** — chlorhexidine is inactivated by sodium lauryl sulfate, which is in many toothpastes.
Chlorhexidine is the right tool for specific situations, not a general daily mouthwash.

### Essential-oil mouthwashes (e.g., Listerine-style formulations)
Contain combinations of essential oils — typically eucalyptol, menthol, methyl salicylate, and thymol — at fixed concentrations. Reasonable evidence for plaque and gingivitis reduction with twice-daily use. Suitable for longer-term daily use.
- **Sometimes contain alcohol** at concentrations that can be irritating; alcohol-free versions exist (see `product-mouthwash-alcohol-free`).
- **Strong taste** that some users find unpleasant.
- **Useful as a daily adjunct** alongside good brushing for users with persistent mild gingivitis or higher plaque risk.

### Cetylpyridinium chloride (CPC) mouthwashes
A quaternary ammonium antiseptic, milder than chlorhexidine and intended for daily use. Modest evidence for plaque reduction. Usually well-tolerated. Often combined with other ingredients in over-the-counter rinses.


## Who this category suits
Antiseptic mouthwash may be appropriate for:
- **Users whose report flagged Moderate or Advanced plaque**, particularly when bleeding gums (gingivitis) are present alongside.
- **Users in the immediate aftermath of a professional cleaning, scaling and root planing, or periodontal surgery** — chlorhexidine is commonly prescribed for two weeks post-procedure.
- **Users undergoing orthodontic treatment** with fixed appliances, where cleaning is harder and plaque accumulation higher.
- **Users with persistent gingivitis** despite good home care, as a short-term adjunct while addressing underlying causes.
- **Users with implants** experiencing peri-implant inflammation (peri-mucositis) — under dentist guidance.
It is *not* a routine daily product for users with healthy gums, low plaque accumulation, and no specific indication.


## How to use
- **Follow the product's stated duration** — chlorhexidine typically two to four weeks at a time; daily-use products like CPC or essential-oil rinses can be used long-term.
- **Use at a different time from fluoride toothpaste** — at minimum 30 minutes apart. Mouthwashes can interact with toothpaste ingredients in ways that reduce effectiveness.
- **Swish for the full 30–60 seconds** as the product directs, then spit; don't swallow.
- **Don't eat or drink for 30 minutes afterwards** to allow the antiseptic to work.


## What antiseptic mouthwash is not
A few clarifications worth being explicit about:
- It does not replace brushing. The mechanical removal of plaque by the brush is what does the cleaning work; antiseptic action is supportive.
- It does not "kill 99% of germs" in any meaningful sense — and even if it did, the bacteria re-establish within hours. The clinical question is whether the cumulative reduction translates to less plaque and gum inflammation, and the answer varies by product and use pattern.
- It does not treat gum disease on its own. For Moderate or Advanced plaque-related gum disease, a course of professional treatment (scaling, possibly scaling and root planing) is the foundation; mouthwash supports it.

# Fluoride mouthwash
A daily fluoride mouthwash is a low-concentration fluoride rinse (typically 225 ppm) used as an additional cavity-prevention tool. It works by giving teeth an extra exposure to fluoride at a different time of day from brushing, which extends the window of fluoride protection.
For users at typical cavity risk who already brush twice a day with fluoride toothpaste, fluoride mouthwash is a useful enhancer rather than essential. For users at higher risk — Advanced Cavity findings, exposed root surfaces from gum recession, dry mouth, or extensive dental work — it can meaningfully reduce new cavity formation.


## Who this category suits
Daily fluoride mouthwash suits:
- **Users whose report flagged an Advanced Cavity** and who want additional cavity protection on top of standard fluoride toothpaste.
- **Users with Moderate or Advanced gum recession**, where exposed root surfaces are more vulnerable to root caries.
- **Users with dry mouth** from medication, medical conditions, or cancer treatment, where reduced saliva increases cavity risk.
- **Users with extensive crowns, bridges, implants, or fillings** where margins between dental work and natural tooth need extra protection.
- **Users in active orthodontic treatment** with fixed appliances that complicate cleaning.
- **Older adults**, particularly those with gum recession and root-surface exposure.
For most adults at typical risk who already brush effectively with fluoride toothpaste, daily fluoride mouthwash adds incremental rather than transformative protection.


## What to look for
- **Sodium fluoride at approximately 225 ppm**, which is the standard daily-use concentration. This is on the pack.
- **Alcohol-free formulation** if you have a sensitive mouth, gum recession, or dry mouth — alcohol can be irritating and worsens dry mouth. See `product-mouthwash-alcohol-free` for general alcohol-free options.
- **Daily-use suitability** — most fluoride mouthwashes are formulated for daily use. A few are formulated as weekly higher-strength rinses (typically 900 ppm) for cavity-active patients on dentist guidance.


## How to use
The single most important habit is *when* you use it:
- **Use it at a different time of day from brushing.** Common pattern: brush morning and night, rinse with fluoride mouthwash after lunch (or any time mid-day). Using mouthwash immediately after brushing tends to wash off the higher-concentration toothpaste fluoride before it has time to work — counterproductive.
- **Use the recommended amount** (typically 10 ml — about two teaspoons), swish vigorously for 60 seconds, and spit. Don't swallow.
- **Don't eat or drink for 30 minutes afterwards** to maintain fluoride contact.
- **Don't dilute it** with water before use.


## When fluoride mouthwash isn't enough
If you have an active high-risk pattern — multiple recent cavities, severe dry mouth, post-radiation oral health needs — a daily fluoride mouthwash on top of standard toothpaste may not be sufficient. In those cases, a dentist may recommend:
- Prescription-strength fluoride toothpaste (see `product-toothpaste-prescription-fluoride`)
- A higher-strength fluoride mouthwash (typically prescription)
- In-clinic fluoride varnish applications at recall visits
- Saliva substitutes or saliva-stimulating products for dry mouth (see `product-dry-mouth-products` if added)


## Mouthwash is not a replacement for brushing or flossing
A frequently helpful clarification: mouthwash does not remove plaque the way brushing does. A 60-second rinse cannot mechanically dislodge biofilm from tooth surfaces. Fluoride mouthwash is a *protective enhancement*, not a cleaning method. Skipping brushing in favour of mouthwash is not a viable substitute.

# Night guard (occlusal splint)
A night guard — clinically called an *occlusal splint* — is a thin, custom-fitted plastic appliance worn over the upper or lower teeth, typically at night, to protect the teeth from grinding (*bruxism*) or clenching forces. For users whose screening report has flagged signs of occlusal wear, or for users whose dentist has identified bruxism as a contributing factor to gum recession or other findings, a night guard is one of the more useful preventive tools available.
The most important thing to know about night guards: **a properly fitted night guard requires a dentist's involvement.** Over-the-counter "boil-and-bite" guards have a place — short-term protection while waiting for a custom guard, or for users with very mild grinding — but they do not replace a dentist-fitted custom appliance for established bruxism.


## Who this category suits
A night guard may be appropriate for users who:
- **Show signs of grinding or clenching** — flattened or worn-looking tooth surfaces, fractured fillings, sensitivity, jaw pain or stiffness on waking, headaches that start in the temples or jaw.
- **Have been told by a dentist** that they are bruxers or have a heavy bite.
- **Have gum recession** that is suspected to involve excessive bite forces alongside other contributors.
- **Have multiple crowns, veneers, or other extensive dental work** that needs protection from grinding forces.
- **Have a partner who reports hearing them grind their teeth at night.**
A night guard is *not* appropriate as a self-prescribed solution for vague tooth or jaw symptoms. Pain, sudden bite changes, or persistent jaw stiffness should be assessed by a dentist before assuming bruxism is the cause.



## Custom-fitted vs over-the-counter
### Custom-fitted (dentist-made)
The dentist takes detailed impressions or digital scans of your teeth, and the guard is made at a dental laboratory to fit the exact shape of your teeth. Typically:
- **Best fit and durability.** A well-made custom guard can last many years.
- **Most comfortable.** Designed not to interfere with breathing or speech.
- **Adjusted for your bite.** A dentist checks how your teeth meet against the guard and refines the fit so the bite forces are distributed properly.
- **Most expensive.** Costs in Singapore vary by clinic and material — typically several hundred dollars.
- **Recommended for established bruxism, extensive dental work, or persistent jaw symptoms.**

### Over-the-counter "boil-and-bite"
Available at pharmacies and some retailers. The user softens the guard in hot water, then bites into it to shape it to their teeth. Typically:
- **Affordable** — usually a small fraction of the cost of a custom guard.
- **Reasonable for short-term use** — for example, while waiting for a custom guard appointment, or as a trial to see whether wearing a guard at all is acceptable.
- **Less durable** — often needs replacing within months.
- **Less comfortable** — bulkier, can interfere with sleep, and the bite is not adjusted by a dentist.
- **Not appropriate for established bruxism, multiple crowns, or persistent symptoms.** A poorly fitting guard can sometimes worsen jaw symptoms.

### Stock guards (one-size-fits-all)
Sometimes sold in pharmacies. Generally not recommended — fit is poor, and a poorly fitting guard is uncomfortable enough that most users abandon it within days.


## What a night guard does and doesn't do
- **It does:** absorb and distribute grinding forces so the wear happens to the plastic guard instead of the enamel; protect existing crowns, veneers, and fillings; reduce jaw muscle activity in some users; relieve some types of jaw pain.
- **It doesn't:** stop grinding from happening. The guard is a *protective* device, not a treatment for the underlying habit. Stress, sleep position, sleep disorders, and other factors that drive bruxism are addressed separately.
- **It doesn't fix gum recession, cavities, or worn fillings.** Those need their own treatment; the guard prevents further damage.


## Care
- Rinse with cool water after use (hot water can warp the plastic).
- Store in the case provided, dry, away from sunlight.
- Clean weekly with a denture cleaner or as the dentist recommends.
- Bring it to your dental check-ups so the dentist can inspect for wear.
- A custom guard typically lasts five to ten years; replace sooner if you can see meaningful wear marks on the surface.

# Electric toothbrush
An electric toothbrush — particularly an oscillating-rotating or sonic model — is one of the higher-impact upgrades available to most adults' oral hygiene routine. Independent reviews consistently show that powered brushes remove more plaque than manual brushes, and the difference is largest for the people who tend to need it most: those with high plaque accumulation, limited dexterity, or a habit of brushing too hard.

## Who this category suits
Electric toothbrushes are particularly recommended for:
- **Users whose report flagged Moderate or Advanced plaque.** Electric brushes typically achieve better plaque removal at the gum line, which is where most plaque-related issues start.
- **Users with crowded, restored, or hard-to-clean teeth.** Crowns, bridges, implants, and orthodontic work all benefit from the more thorough plaque disruption of a powered brush.
- **Users with arthritis or limited dexterity.** The brush does the technique work; the user just guides it.
- **Users who consistently press too hard with a manual brush.** Most modern electric brushes include a pressure sensor that lights up or pulses when pressure is excessive — a useful behavioural prompt that's hard to replicate manually.
- **Users with gum recession who need to lighten their pressure.** A sensitive-mode electric brush often makes it easier to brush effectively *without* damaging the gum margin.


## The two main types
### Oscillating-rotating brushes
The brush head rotates back and forth in short bursts, often pulsing to disrupt plaque. The most clinically validated category. Smaller round heads typically focus on one tooth at a time.

- **Best for:** users new to electric brushing; users with crowded teeth where the small round head reaches well; users wanting the strongest evidence-base.

### Sonic brushes
The brush head vibrates at very high frequency in a small side-to-side motion, with the vibration creating fluid dynamics that help disrupt plaque slightly beyond where the bristles directly touch.
- **Best for:** users who prefer a more familiar back-and-forth feel; users with gum recession or sensitivity (sonic brushes tend to feel gentler in sensitive mode); users with bridges or implants where the fluid-dynamics action helps clean around restorations.
For most users, the difference between the two types is smaller than the difference between *any* electric brush and a manual brush used poorly. Either type is a reasonable choice.



## Features worth having
- **Two-minute built-in timer** with 30-second quadrant prompts — encourages brushing for the recommended duration with even coverage.
- **Pressure sensor** that visibly indicates when you are pressing too hard. Particularly useful for users with gum recession.
- **Sensitive mode** for users with gum recession or general sensitivity.
- **Replaceable head** — the brush body lasts years; only the head needs replacing every three months.
Premium features (Bluetooth connectivity, app coaching, multiple modes) are nice but rarely change clinical outcomes. The four features above cover what matters.


## Replacement
Replace the brush head every three months, or sooner if the bristles splay or appear frayed. Don't be tempted to stretch this — a worn head undermines the whole benefit of a powered brush.


## When a manual brush is fine
An electric brush is not mandatory. Users with low plaque accumulation, healthy gums, and effective manual technique do not need to switch — a soft manual brush used well is suitable. The main candidates for the upgrade are those whose findings or circumstances suggest manual brushing isn't keeping up. See `product-toothbrush-manual-soft`.

# Manual soft-bristle toothbrush
A manual soft-bristle toothbrush is the simplest, most accessible, and most universally appropriate oral hygiene tool. For everyday use, soft bristles are the right choice for almost every adult — and especially so for anyone whose screening report flagged gum recession, plaque accumulation, or sensitivity.


## Who this category suits
Soft-bristle manual brushes suit:
- **Anyone whose report flagged gum recession** at any severity. Medium or hard bristles accelerate recession and damage exposed root surfaces.
- **Anyone whose report flagged plaque** at Initial or Moderate severity. Soft bristles, used with the right technique for the full two minutes, remove plaque effectively without damaging gum tissue.
- **Users with sensitive teeth** who find electric brushes too intense.
- **Users who travel often or want a backup brush** — manual brushes are inexpensive and don't need charging.
- **Users who prefer a manual brush** for personal or budget reasons. A soft manual brush used properly is a reasonable lifelong choice.


## What to look for
- **Soft or extra-soft bristles only.** Medium and hard bristles are not appropriate for adult daily use; they cause more harm than benefit.
- **A small to compact head** that can reach the back of the mouth and the surfaces of individual teeth. Oversized heads brush in broad strokes that miss areas.
- **A non-slip handle** that you can grip comfortably for two minutes without strain.
- **Tapered or rounded bristle tips** that are gentle on the gum margin while reaching slightly under it.
Brand differences within the soft-bristle category are minor for most users. Almost any soft-bristle brush from a reputable manufacturer will perform well; choose based on head size, handle comfort, and whatever encourages you to brush for the full two minutes.


## Replacement
Replace the brush every three months, or sooner if the bristles splay outwards or look frayed. Splayed bristles indicate the brush is no longer cleaning effectively — and often that you have been pressing too hard.


## When to consider an electric brush instead
While a manual soft-bristle brush is suitable for almost everyone, an electric toothbrush typically removes more plaque per minute of brushing for most users — particularly people with arthritis, limited dexterity, crowded teeth, or a history of high plaque accumulation despite consistent manual brushing. See `product-toothbrush-electric` for that category.

# Desensitising toothpaste
A desensitising toothpaste is a fluoride toothpaste that contains additional active ingredients designed to reduce sensitivity. It is particularly useful for users whose screening report flagged gum recession (which exposes the more sensitive root surface) or who experience sharp short-lived pain in response to cold drinks, cold air, sweet foods, or brushing.
Desensitising toothpaste is best thought of as an *additional* layer of protection rather than a replacement for understanding *why* the sensitivity is happening. Mild root sensitivity from gum recession often responds well to desensitising toothpaste. Sensitivity from other causes — a cavity, a cracked tooth, exposed nerve from extensive decay, or recent dental work — needs clinical attention regardless of which toothpaste you use.


## Who this category suits
Desensitising toothpaste suits:
- **Users whose report flagged Moderate or Advanced gum recession**, particularly those experiencing sensitivity to cold or sweet foods.
- **Users with generalised tooth sensitivity** without an obvious cavity or other clinical cause.
- **Users in the recovery period after a deep cleaning** or periodontal treatment, where sensitivity often increases temporarily.
- **Users with mild enamel erosion** from acidic foods or drinks where the dentine is becoming exposed.
Desensitising toothpaste is *not* a substitute for clinical assessment when sensitivity is severe, persistent despite good care, localised to a single tooth, or accompanied by pain on biting or sweetness — these patterns suggest a problem that toothpaste alone won't address.


## What to look for
Two main active-ingredient categories work by different mechanisms:
- **Potassium nitrate formulations.** Calm the nerve response inside the tooth by reducing nerve excitability. Effects build up over two to four weeks of consistent twice-daily use.
- **Stannous fluoride or arginine-calcium-carbonate formulations.** Physically block the small tubules in the dentine that conduct sensitivity signals. Some formulations claim faster relief (within a few days) but evidence varies.
Both work well for most users. Practical features to look for:
- **Fluoride content of 1,000–1,500 ppm** — desensitising toothpastes should still provide standard cavity protection. Some specialty desensitising products are lower in fluoride; check the pack.
- **Daily-use suitability** — most desensitising toothpastes are fine for ongoing daily use. A few are formulated as short-term courses.
- **Mild flavour** that you can tolerate twice a day, every day. Compliance matters.


## How to use
- **Use it twice a day in place of your regular toothpaste**, not as an addition.
- **Be patient** — most desensitising toothpastes take two to four weeks of consistent twice-daily use before the effect peaks. Stopping after a few days because "it's not working" is the most common reason people don't see benefit.
- **Continue indefinitely if it works.** Stopping a desensitising toothpaste once you've stopped feeling sensitivity will often see the sensitivity return after a few weeks.
- **Apply directly to the sensitive area** for an extra-targeted effect: after brushing, put a small amount of toothpaste on a clean fingertip and rub it into the sensitive root surface, leaving it on overnight. (Don't do this with prescription-strength formulations without dentist guidance.)


## When desensitising toothpaste isn't enough
If sensitivity persists or worsens despite four weeks of consistent use, see a dentist. Reasons a dentist may recommend escalating:
- A clinical examination to rule out a cavity, crack, or pulp inflammation
- Application of professional fluoride varnish, which can give faster and more durable relief than home toothpaste alone
- For severe localised sensitivity from gum recession, a *gum graft* or other surgical procedure to cover the exposed root surface
- A bite assessment, if grinding or clenching is contributing

# Standard fluoride toothpaste
A fluoride toothpaste at 1,000–1,500 ppm is the baseline cavity-prevention tool for adults at typical risk. The clinical evidence for fluoride toothpaste is extremely strong — it is the single most well-evidenced cavity-prevention measure in dentistry — and the choice between brands at this concentration matters far less than simply using one consistently, twice a day, with the right technique.


## Who this category suits
Standard fluoride toothpaste suits:
- **Almost all adults at typical risk.** This is the default product, and most people should use it.
- **Users whose report flagged plaque** at any severity. Fluoride toothpaste does not remove plaque (the mechanical action of the brush does that), but it strengthens the enamel against the acid attacks that follow.
- **Users with no specific need for a higher-strength or specialty toothpaste.** Most adults sit here.


## What to look for
- **1,000–1,500 ppm fluoride.** This must be on the pack and is required by regulation. Products marketed as "natural" or "fluoride-free" generally don't meet this.
- **Sodium fluoride or sodium monofluorophosphate** as the active ingredient. Both work well.
- **No specific need for whitening or anti-tartar formulations** unless your dentist has recommended them. These are usually marketing differentiators rather than clinical necessities.
- **A formulation that doesn't taste so unpleasant that you avoid using it for the full two minutes.** Compliance matters more than minor formulation differences.
The differences between major brands at this concentration are small and unlikely to translate to noticeable differences in oral health. Choose one you'll use consistently.


## How to use
- **Pea-sized amount.** More toothpaste does not mean more cleaning; it just means more rinsing.
- **Twice daily** — morning and last thing at night.
- **Spit, don't rinse with water.** This is the single most underused habit in oral care. Leaving the residual film of toothpaste on the teeth significantly extends the fluoride contact time.
- **Don't eat or drink for 30 minutes after brushing**, particularly at night, to maintain fluoride contact.


## When you might need a different toothpaste
A standard fluoride toothpaste is not appropriate for some specific situations — see the relevant entries:
- **Sensitive teeth** that haven't responded to good technique alone — see `product-toothpaste-desensitising`
- **High cavity risk** (multiple recent cavities, extensive crowns or implants, dry mouth, post-radiation): a higher-strength prescription toothpaste (2,800 or 5,000 ppm) — see `product-toothpaste-prescription-fluoride`
- **Children**, particularly those younger than three: lower-fluoride children's toothpastes are appropriate
- **Specific medical conditions** that warrant tailored formulations — discuss with your dentist or doctor


## On whitening, charcoal, and "natural" toothpastes
A few notes worth being clear about:
- **Whitening toothpastes** typically work by mechanical abrasion (removing surface stains) rather than chemical bleaching. They can be more abrasive than standard formulations and are not ideal for users with gum recession or sensitivity. Effects on intrinsic tooth colour are limited.
- **Charcoal toothpastes** are abrasive and lack robust evidence of benefit. Most do not contain fluoride, which means their use comes at the cost of cavity protection. Not recommended.
- **"Natural" or fluoride-free toothpastes** vary widely; the key concern is that they typically lack fluoride, which is the single most evidenced cavity-prevention ingredient. If you want a fluoride-free option for personal reasons, discuss it with your dentist — alternative remineralisation strategies exist (such as hydroxyapatite formulations) but they have less evidence.

# Prescription-strength fluoride toothpaste
Prescription fluoride toothpastes contain significantly higher fluoride concentrations than over-the-counter toothpastes — typically 2,800 ppm or 5,000 ppm, compared to the 1,000–1,500 ppm in standard formulations. They are reserved for adults at high cavity risk and are available only with a prescription from a dentist or doctor.


## Who this category suits
Prescription fluoride toothpaste may be appropriate for adults with:
- **A history of multiple recent cavities** despite good home care
- **Extensive dental work** — multiple crowns, bridges, implants, or large fillings that create harder-to-clean surfaces
- **Exposed root surfaces** from gum recession, which are more cavity-prone than enamel
- **Reduced saliva flow** (xerostomia) from medication, certain medical conditions, or cancer treatment — saliva is one of the body's main cavity-prevention tools, so reduced flow significantly increases risk
- **Post-radiation therapy to the head and neck**, which causes prolonged dry mouth and elevated cavity risk
- **Active orthodontic treatment with fixed appliances** (braces), which create areas where plaque accumulates
- **A clinically assessed high cavity risk** following a dentist's evaluation
If your screening report flagged an Advanced Cavity, ask your dentist whether prescription-strength fluoride is appropriate for your situation. It is not standard for everyone with one cavity, but it can be a useful tool when the underlying risk pattern warrants it.


## What it does
Prescription-strength fluoride works by the same mechanism as standard fluoride toothpaste — strengthening enamel and supporting remineralisation — but at concentrations that drive faster and deeper effects. The clinical evidence shows reduced cavity rates compared to standard toothpaste in high-risk groups.
It does *not* reverse existing cavities — those still need clinical treatment. It does help arrest very early enamel demineralisation, slow the formation of new cavities, and protect exposed root surfaces.


## How it differs from standard toothpaste
- **Higher fluoride concentration:** typically 2,800 ppm or 5,000 ppm (vs 1,000–1,500 ppm in standard).
- **Prescription required.** Available only via a dentist's or doctor's prescription, dispensed by a pharmacy or some dental clinics.
- **Used in place of regular toothpaste**, twice daily, not as an addition.
- **Pea-sized amount, spit don't rinse** — same as standard toothpaste, the spit-don't-rinse rule matters even more here.
- **Not for children.** The higher fluoride content makes it inappropriate for young children, who should use age-appropriate fluoride toothpaste only.
- **Generally not for people with severe kidney disease** — discuss with the prescriber.


## How long to use it
The duration depends on the underlying risk:
- **Active cavity-risk period** (during orthodontic treatment, post-radiation recovery, after a cluster of cavities): typically used until the dentist re-assesses risk, often 3–12 months.
- **Ongoing high risk** (chronic dry mouth, multiple implants, longstanding root-surface exposure): may be used long-term as part of a maintenance routine.
The dentist will reassess at each visit and adjust the prescription accordingly.


## What it doesn't replace
Prescription-strength fluoride toothpaste is one tool in a wider set. It works best alongside:
- Effective brushing technique and consistent twice-daily use
- Daily interdental cleaning
- Reduced sugar and acid frequency in the diet
- Professional cleaning at the recommended interval
- In some cases, in-clinic fluoride varnish applications
A prescription toothpaste alone, without addressing the underlying risk drivers, achieves much less than a comprehensive routine.

# Water flosser
A water flosser (also called an *oral irrigator*) is a device that directs a thin pulsating jet of water between teeth and along the gum line. It removes loose plaque, food debris, and bacteria from areas that floss and interdental brushes can also reach — and a few that they reach less well, particularly around bridges, implants, and braces.
Water flossers are particularly useful for users whose dental anatomy or appliances make traditional flossing difficult, and for users who simply find them easier and more pleasant — which translates to actually using them daily.


## Who this category suits
Water flossers are particularly recommended for:
- **Users with bridges, crowns, or implants**, where the water jet cleans around restorations more thoroughly than floss can.
- **Users with braces or other fixed orthodontic appliances**, which create many areas where floss is awkward.
- **Users with limited dexterity** — arthritis, hand injury, or any reason that makes spool floss or interdental brushes difficult.
- **Users with periodontal pockets** that have formed around teeth with gum disease — water flossers can flush bacteria from pockets up to a certain depth.
- **Users with a strong gag reflex** that interferes with using floss or interdental brushes at the back of the mouth.
- **Users who travel often** — countertop water flossers don't travel well, but cordless rechargeable models are practical.
- **Users who simply prefer the experience** and will use it consistently.


## What a water flosser is and isn't
A few clarifications:
- A water flosser is **not a substitute for brushing**. The mechanical action of the brush is what removes plaque from the front, back, and chewing surfaces of teeth. Water flossing is for *between* teeth and at the gum line.
- A water flosser is **a reasonable alternative or addition to traditional floss**, not a replacement for it in every case. It removes loose plaque and debris well, but does not physically scrape the tooth surface the way floss or interdental brushes do.
- For users with healthy gums and tight tooth contacts, traditional floss can be more thorough at the actual contact point between teeth. For users with bridges, implants, or wider gaps, water flossing is often equal or better.
- Many dentists now suggest water flossing as a complement, not a replacement, for users with mixed natural teeth and restorations.


## Types
- **Countertop models** — sit on the bathroom counter, plug into mains power, have a larger water reservoir (refilling less often), typically a wider range of pressure settings, and a range of attachments.
- **Cordless / rechargeable models** — handheld, smaller reservoir (refill more often), portable. Slightly less powerful than countertop models in most cases. Better for travel.
- **Shower-mounted models** — connect to the shower or sink water supply. Less common; can be useful for users with limited counter space.
For daily home use, a countertop model is the typical choice. A cordless model is the better choice for users who travel often or have limited bathroom space.


## What to look for
- **Multiple pressure settings.** Start at the lowest setting and increase gradually. Higher pressure is not better — it can damage gum tissue. A "sensitive" or low setting is essential for users with gum recession or after periodontal treatment.
- **At least one specialised attachment** — a periodontal tip (for users with gum disease, with a soft rubber tip that goes slightly under the gum line), or an orthodontic tip (with bristles for users with braces).
- **Sufficient reservoir capacity** to clean the whole mouth without refilling — typically 600 ml or more for a countertop model.
- **Sturdy build** — the unit will sit on a wet bathroom counter for years.


## How to use
1. **Fill the reservoir** with lukewarm water (cold water can be uncomfortable, especially with gum recession).
2. **Choose the right tip** — standard for most users, periodontal for those with gum pockets, orthodontic for braces.
3. **Lean over the sink** with the unit ready and your mouth slightly open.
4. **Place the tip just at the gum line** between two teeth, angled slightly toward the gum.
5. **Turn on at the lowest pressure setting** and let the water run between teeth, holding the tip in place for a couple of seconds at each gap.
6. **Move slowly along the dental arch**, pausing at each gap.
7. **The whole mouth typically takes 60–90 seconds** per session.
Use once daily, ideally before brushing, so any debris loosened by the water flosser is then brushed away.


## Cautions
- Don't use a water flosser at maximum pressure if you have gum recession or bleeding gums — start gentle and build up as the gums adapt.
- If you have a recent extraction, surgical site, or healing gum graft, follow your dentist's specific guidance before using a water flosser around the area.
- A water flosser does not replace professional cleaning at the recommended interval.
