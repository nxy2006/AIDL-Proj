# Your report says: OVERALL RISK — HIGH

The canonical summary text generated by the Oral Link screening engine for a High overall risk is:

> *Photos classified under High Risk show significant dental issues requiring immediate attention. This includes advanced decay, severe gum disease or substantial tooth damage. Immediate treatment is crucial to mitigate pain, prevent further damage, or infections, and restore oral health. A comprehensive dental examination is recommended to determine the specific treatments needed. Delay in addressing these concerns can lead to more severe health issues and complications.*

A High overall risk means the screening has identified findings that warrant prompt dental attention — typically Advanced-severity findings on one or more teeth, often combined with multiple Moderate or Initial findings elsewhere in the mouth. This is the most urgent of the three risk classifications the screening can give.

## What this typically reflects

A High risk classification usually reflects one or more of the following:

- An Advanced Cavity on a specific tooth (substantial decay that is unlikely to be managed with a simple filling)
- Advanced gum recession with significant root exposure, possibly accompanied by signs of underlying gum disease
- Multiple missing teeth that affect chewing function, bite stability, or aesthetics
- Heavy plaque or calculus accumulation across many teeth
- A combination of any of the above

A High risk is not necessarily an emergency in the medical sense — but it is a clear signal that the conditions identified are unlikely to resolve without professional treatment, and that delaying treatment is likely to make matters worse.

## What to do next

A High-risk result calls for prompt action:

1. **Schedule a dental appointment within the next one to two weeks.** This is not a recall-visit timeline — the visit should be booked specifically to assess the screening findings.
2. **Look at the Potential Actions table on your report.** It lists the specific treatments the screening suggests, prioritised. High-priority items in that table are the ones to discuss first with your dentist.
3. **Expect the visit to take longer than a routine check-up.** Your dentist may take X-rays, do a full periodontal assessment, and discuss a treatment plan that may unfold across several appointments.
4. **Be prepared for the possibility of specialist referral.** Depending on the specific findings — particularly Advanced gum recession or extensive decay — your dentist may refer you to a periodontist, prosthodontist, or other specialist.
5. **Bring your screening report and the Potential Actions list with you.** They give your dentist a useful starting point.

## In an emergency

If you are currently experiencing severe pain, swelling that affects your breathing or swallowing, uncontrolled bleeding from the mouth, or facial trauma, do not wait for a routine appointment. ## What this does *not* mean

High overall risk does not mean:

- You will lose teeth
- You need major surgery
- All the recommended treatments must be completed at once
- The findings cannot be managed — most can be, with timely care

It does mean that the screening has flagged conditions that should be assessed and managed promptly. Many High-risk situations stabilise well once the most urgent items are addressed.

# What "High / Medium / Low priority" mean on your report

The "Potential Actions" section near the end of your screening report lists suggested treatments — for example, Crown, Cleaning, or Partial Denture — alongside a priority label for each. Priority is colour-coded: High in red/pink, Low in blue, with Medium in between.

Priority describes **how urgently the suggested treatment should be considered**, not how serious the underlying finding is. The two are related but not the same.

## High priority

A High-priority recommendation means the screening has identified a condition where delaying the treatment is likely to make things worse — either through pain, infection risk, or further damage to the tooth or surrounding tissue. These are the items you would discuss with a dentist as soon as practical, typically within days to a couple of weeks rather than at a routine check-up months from now.

Examples of when a High priority is typical: an Advanced Cavity on a specific tooth (which may need a filling, root canal, or crown depending on extent), substantial structural damage, or a condition where escalation to specialist care is likely.

## Medium priority

A Medium-priority recommendation indicates a treatment that should be addressed in a defined window — generally weeks to a few months — but is not as time-sensitive as a High-priority item. Most Moderate-severity findings that have a clear treatment pathway sit here. These are items you would actively schedule rather than fold into a routine recall visit.

## Low priority

A Low-priority recommendation is one that should be done, but does not need to be rushed. Routine professional cleaning typically falls here, even when overall risk is High — because cleaning is part of long-term oral health management rather than urgent intervention. A partial denture for a long-standing missing tooth often appears as Low priority for the same reason: the tooth has been missing for some time, so a few more weeks of planning the replacement does not change the outcome.

## Why a Low-priority recommendation can appear on a High-risk report

It is common to see High overall risk *and* Low-priority recommendations on the same report. The two are measuring different things:

- **Overall risk** is the screening's view of your dental health as a whole, taking all findings together.
- **Priority** is per recommended treatment, and reflects how time-sensitive that specific treatment is.

If your report shows High overall risk, a High-priority Crown recommendation, and a Low-priority Cleaning, the message is: address the Crown promptly, and schedule the Cleaning when convenient — not that the Cleaning is unimportant.

## Priority is a suggestion, not a clinical instruction

The priority label is generated by the screening algorithm based on the findings it identified. A dentist's in-person assessment may agree, accelerate, or de-escalate any of these recommendations after examining the actual condition of each tooth. Use the priority labels as a guide for the conversation with your dentist, not as a fixed treatment plan.

# What "Initial / Moderate / Advanced" mean on your report

Your Oral Link screening report uses a three-band severity scale to describe how far each flagged condition has progressed on a specific tooth. The scale is the same across all findings — the same Initial / Moderate / Advanced labels apply whether the finding is plaque, gum recession, or any other condition the screening identifies.

Each band is colour-coded on the dental chart at the top of your report:

- **Initial — light blue marker.** The condition has been identified at an early stage. Visible signs are present but limited. Practical impact on your day-to-day comfort or function is usually minimal.
- **Moderate — orange marker.** The condition has progressed beyond early signs. There may be visible changes that you can see in the mirror, and you may notice symptoms (sensitivity, mild discomfort, or aesthetic changes) starting to appear.
- **Advanced — red marker.** The condition is well established. Visible signs are clear, symptoms are likely, and the condition is more likely to require professional dental care to manage.

## How severity should shape your response

Severity is not a strict timeline — different findings progress at different rates, and the same severity band can mean different urgency for different conditions. The general pattern is:

- **Initial findings** are best addressed through routine dental care and good home oral hygiene. They rarely demand a dedicated dental visit on their own.
- **Moderate findings** typically warrant a dental review on a defined timeline (weeks rather than months) so the underlying cause can be examined and managed.
- **Advanced findings** generally warrant prompt dental review — the timeline shortens, and specialist input becomes more likely depending on the specific finding.

Your report may flag the same finding at different severities on different teeth — for example, gum recession at Initial on one tooth and Moderate on another. In that case the more advanced finding is what drives your overall urgency.

## Severity is per finding, per tooth

It's worth noting that severity is set independently for each (tooth, finding) combination. A tooth may have one finding at Initial severity and another at Advanced severity — the colour markers on the dental chart visualise this for the most urgent finding on each tooth.

## Severity does not change the diagnosis status

A screening identifies *the appearance of* a condition; it is not a clinical diagnosis. Even an Advanced finding on the screening still requires a dentist's in-person examination to confirm what is actually happening and what (if any) treatment is appropriate.

# How tooth numbers work on your screening report

Your Oral Link screening report identifies each tooth using the Universal Numbering System (UNS) — a standard scheme that numbers adult teeth from 1 to 32. Each entry in your report's findings table looks like:

> `1st Bicuspid (Tooth 5, Upper Right)`

That single line tells you three things: the tooth's anatomical name (1st Bicuspid), its UNS number (5), and which side of your mouth it sits on (Upper Right).

## How the numbers are arranged

In the Universal Numbering System, numbering starts at the back of your upper-right jaw and runs along the top arch to the back of your upper-left jaw, then drops down to the lower-left and runs along the bottom arch back to the lower-right.

- **Upper jaw, right to left:** Tooth 1 (back-right wisdom tooth) → Tooth 16 (back-left wisdom tooth)
- **Lower jaw, left to right:** Tooth 17 (back-left wisdom tooth) → Tooth 32 (back-right wisdom tooth)

Some quick anchors:

- **Tooth 8 and Tooth 9** are your two upper front teeth (central incisors)
- **Tooth 24 and Tooth 25** are your two lower front teeth (central incisors)
- **Tooth 1, 16, 17, and 32** are the four wisdom teeth (if you still have them)
- **Tooth 6 and Tooth 11** are your upper canines ("eye teeth")

## Why your dentist may use different numbers

Singapore dentists often use a different numbering system called the FDI World Dental Federation system, which uses two-digit numbers (11–48). The two systems describe the same teeth, just with different labels. If you bring your Oral Link report to a dental appointment, your dentist can map between the two without difficulty — the anatomical name in brackets (e.g. `1st Bicuspid`) is the same in either system.

## Quadrant labels

Your report also names the quadrant — Upper Right, Upper Left, Lower Right, or Lower Left. "Right" and "Left" refer to *your* right and left as the patient, not the camera's perspective. So `Tooth 5, Upper Right` is on the right side of your mouth as you look in a mirror, even though it appears on the *left* of the dental chart in the report (because the chart shows your mouth as the dentist sees it).

# Advanced cavity — what your screening identified

Your screening report has flagged an Advanced Cavity on one or more teeth. A cavity (also called *caries* or *tooth decay*) is a hole in the tooth caused by acid-producing bacteria eroding the enamel and the underlying tooth structure. An *advanced* cavity is one where the decay has progressed beyond the surface — typically deep enough that a simple filling may not be sufficient, and where prompt treatment is needed to prevent further damage or infection.

A note about the labelling: the Oral Link screening engine reports cavities under the single name "Advanced Cavity" rather than separating them into Initial / Moderate / Advanced bands the way it does for plaque or gum recession. This is because cavities can be very difficult to identify reliably from photographs alone in their earliest stages — they often start between teeth or under existing fillings where photos cannot reach. The screening flags only those cavities visible enough in the photographs to be confident about, which by the time they are that visible, are typically already advanced.

This means: an absence of Advanced Cavity findings on your report does *not* mean you have no cavities — it means none were visible at the level of confidence the screening uses to flag them. Smaller cavities are detected by clinical examination and X-rays at a dental visit.

## What an advanced cavity typically looks like

An advanced cavity may appear as:

- A visible dark hole or pit in the tooth, often brown, grey, or black at the centre
- A clearly broken or missing portion of a tooth
- A larger area of darkened or softened enamel that suggests the decay extends well below the surface
- A tooth that has already partially fractured around an existing decayed area

You may have noticed symptoms such as sensitivity to cold, hot, or sweet foods, a dull or sharp ache that comes and goes, food regularly catching in the affected tooth, or in some cases no symptoms at all (advanced cavities can be surprisingly painless if they have not yet reached the nerve).

## Why advanced cavities matter

Decay does not stop on its own — once bacteria have breached the enamel, they progress into the softer dentine underneath and eventually toward the pulp (the nerve and blood supply at the centre of the tooth). Without treatment:

- The cavity continues to enlarge
- The tooth structure that remains becomes weaker and more prone to fracture
- The bacteria can reach the pulp, causing pulpitis (inflammation) and eventually pulp necrosis (the nerve dies)
- An infection can form at the root tip, leading to a dental abscess — which can cause severe pain and, in rare cases, serious systemic complications
- The treatment options narrow as the decay progresses (a filling at an earlier stage becomes a root canal or extraction at a later stage)

## What to do next

An Advanced Cavity finding warrants prompt dental review:

1. **Book a dental appointment within the next one to two weeks.** This timeline reflects the fact that advanced cavities tend to progress, and that the available treatment options are typically broader and less invasive when addressed sooner.
2. **Be prepared for X-rays.** X-rays show the depth of the cavity in a way that photographs and clinical examination cannot, and they are essential for the dentist to recommend the right treatment.
3. **Expect a treatment plan, not necessarily one appointment.** Depending on how deep the cavity extends, treatment may involve: a filling (if there is enough sound tooth structure remaining), a crown (if the tooth needs more support than a filling can give), a root canal followed by a crown (if the decay has reached the nerve), or in some cases an extraction (if the tooth cannot be saved).
4. **Maintain gentle oral hygiene around the affected tooth** in the meantime. Continue brushing and cleaning between teeth, but avoid putting force on the affected tooth — chew on the other side if it's sensitive, and don't use the affected tooth to bite into hard foods.

## In an emergency

If you experience severe and persistent toothache, facial swelling, fever, swelling that affects swallowing or breathing, or signs of an abscess (a pimple-like bump on the gum near the tooth, sometimes with discharge), do not wait for a routine appointment. Contact a dentist for emergency care or, in significant cases, go to the nearest A&E department.

## What this finding does *not* mean

An Advanced Cavity finding does not automatically mean you will lose the tooth. Many advanced cavities can be successfully restored — the goal at this stage is to act before the situation narrows further. It also does not mean every cavity in your mouth has been identified — only those visible enough to flag from the photographs. A clinical examination is the only way to assess the full picture.

# Gum recession — what your screening identified

Gum recession is when the gum tissue around your teeth pulls back, exposing more of the tooth — and sometimes the root surface underneath. The clinical name for this is *gingival recession*. When your screening report flags gum recession on a tooth, it means the camera picked up signs that the gum line has moved further down (or up, on lower teeth) than where it would normally sit on a healthy tooth.

Most people do not notice gum recession in its early stages because it usually develops slowly and without pain. Common visual signs your screening looks for include:

- A tooth that appears longer than the teeth next to it
- A visible step or notch where the enamel meets the exposed root
- Darker yellowish areas near the gum line (root surface, which is naturally darker than enamel)
- A gum line that is uneven across the front of your mouth

## What typically causes it

Gum recession can have several causes, often acting together:

- **Brushing too hard or with a hard-bristled brush.** This is one of the most common causes, especially on the canine and premolar teeth where people often press hardest.
- **Gum disease (gingivitis or periodontitis).** Long-term plaque and calculus build-up inflames the gums and causes the supporting tissue to recede.
- **Tooth position or bite forces.** Teeth that are crowded or misaligned can sit at angles that put extra stress on the gum margin.
- **Grinding or clenching (bruxism).** Repeated heavy forces over years can contribute to recession on certain teeth.
- **Genetic factors.** Some people simply have thinner gum tissue, which recedes more easily.
- **Smoking.** Smoking impairs gum tissue healing and is associated with greater recession.

## Why it matters

Once gum tissue has receded, it does not naturally grow back. The exposed root surface is softer than enamel and more prone to sensitivity, decay, and further recession if the underlying cause is not addressed. Early identification — which is what your screening is designed for — gives you the best chance to slow or stop the progression.

## What to do next

A screening identifies the *appearance* of recession; only a dentist can examine the surrounding tissue, measure the recession depth, and confirm the cause. Your next step is a dental review so that the cause can be addressed before the recession progresses.

The severity band on your report (Initial, Moderate, or Advanced) tells you how urgent that review is — see the severity-specific entries for what each band means for you.

# Advanced gum recession — what your screening identified

Your screening report has flagged Advanced-stage gum recession on one or more teeth. At this stage a substantial portion of the tooth root is visible, the recession may extend close to or beyond the bone level supporting the tooth, and there is a meaningful risk that the recession is part of an underlying gum-disease process.

## What this means for you

Advanced recession typically presents as:

- A clearly elongated tooth, often noticeably longer than its neighbours
- A large band of exposed root surface, sometimes with a step or notch you can feel with your tongue
- Sensitivity to cold, sweet foods, or air, which may be marked
- Possible looseness in the affected tooth, gum tenderness, bleeding when brushing, or a change in the way your bite feels
- In some cases, a gap or "black triangle" appearing between teeth where the gum used to fill the space

At this stage the exposed root is highly susceptible to root caries, the supporting bone may already have been affected, and the long-term outlook for the tooth depends on prompt and appropriate clinical care.

## What this often signals

Advanced gum recession is rarely caused by toothbrush abrasion alone. It frequently coexists with periodontitis — a chronic, inflammatory gum disease in which the bone and ligament supporting the tooth break down — and may also involve heavy occlusal forces, longstanding plaque and calculus accumulation, or systemic factors such as smoking or poorly controlled diabetes. These underlying causes need to be identified and addressed; treating the recession in isolation is not sufficient.

## What to do next

Advanced gum recession warrants prompt dental review:

1. **Book a dental appointment within the next one to two weeks.** This is no longer a routine timeline — your dentist needs to examine the tooth, measure the recession and pocket depths around adjacent teeth, and assess whether there is underlying gum disease that is still active.
2. **Expect a referral discussion.** Depending on what your dentist finds, you may be referred to a *periodontist* — a specialist in gum and supporting-tissue conditions — who can evaluate whether non-surgical or surgical treatment (including, in selected cases, a gum graft) is appropriate.
3. **Do not delay because the tooth is not painful.** Advanced recession often progresses without much pain right up to the point that the tooth becomes loose. Pain is a poor indicator of urgency at this stage.
4. **Maintain very gentle oral hygiene in the meantime.** Use a soft-bristled brush, avoid scrubbing, and continue interdental cleaning where you can do so without trauma to the gum.

If you experience significant tooth mobility, severe pain, swelling that affects breathing or swallowing, or other emergency symptoms, seek emergency dental or medical care rather than waiting for a routine appointment.

## What this stage does *not* mean

Advanced gum recession does not automatically mean the affected tooth will be lost. With timely periodontal care many advanced cases can be stabilised. It does mean that home management alone is no longer adequate and that specialist input is more likely to be needed than at earlier severity bands.

# Initial gum recession — what your screening identified

Your screening report has flagged Initial-stage gum recession on one or more teeth. This is the earliest band on Oral Link's severity scale and means the gum line shows the early visual signs of pulling back, but the recession is still mild and the underlying tooth root is largely unexposed.

## What this means for you

Initial recession is the most favourable stage to identify the problem because it is often reversible at the cause-level — meaning, with the right adjustments, you can stop the recession from progressing further. The recession itself does not grow back, but at this stage there is usually no significant root exposure, no notable sensitivity, and no immediate functional concern.

The most important question at this stage is *why* it is happening. The cause is usually one of:

- Brushing too hard or with a hard-bristled toothbrush
- Plaque or calculus build-up at the gum line
- A bite or tooth position that puts extra pressure on certain teeth

Identifying and correcting the cause is far more useful than treating the recession itself.

## What to do next

Initial-stage gum recession is not urgent in the sense that it requires immediate clinical intervention. It is, however, a useful early signal — exactly the kind of finding that screening is designed to surface so that you can act before progression.

A reasonable next step is to:

1. Schedule a routine dental check-up within the next three to six months so the cause can be assessed in person.
2. Review your brushing technique — most people benefit from switching to a soft-bristled brush and a gentler, angled brushing motion (commonly called the modified Bass technique).
3. Maintain good plaque control between visits, including interdental cleaning where your dentist or dental therapist recommends it.

Your dentist will measure the recession, identify the likely cause, and advise whether any further follow-up is needed.

## What this stage does *not* mean

Initial gum recession does not mean you need surgery, gum grafts, or specialist referral in most cases. It does not mean you have advanced gum disease. It is an early-warning marker — most useful when paired with a cause-correcting routine.

# Moderate gum recession — what your screening identified

Your screening report has flagged Moderate-stage gum recession on one or more teeth. At this stage the gum line has receded enough that part of the tooth root is becoming visible, the recession is no longer a faint early-stage marker, and you may be starting to notice symptoms.

## What this means for you

Moderate recession typically presents as:

- A visible step or band of darker, yellowish tissue (the root surface) where the enamel meets the gum
- A tooth that looks noticeably longer than the teeth on either side
- Possible sensitivity to cold drinks, cold air, or sweet foods on the affected teeth
- A gum margin that may appear slightly inflamed or recede more on the cheek-side of the tooth

The exposed root surface is softer than enamel, which means it is more prone to sensitivity and to a type of decay called *root caries* if plaque is allowed to sit on it. This is the stage at which the recession starts to have a real practical impact on day-to-day comfort.

## What typically drives it

Moderate recession usually means the underlying cause has been active for some time. The most common drivers are continued aggressive brushing, untreated plaque or calculus build-up at the gum margin, or repeated occlusal stress on a specific tooth. In some cases moderate recession is the visible part of underlying periodontal (gum) disease that needs professional assessment.

## What to do next

Moderate gum recession warrants a dental review on a defined timeline rather than as a routine check-up. A reasonable next step is to:

1. **Book a dental appointment within the next four to six weeks.** Your dentist will measure the recession depth, examine the surrounding tissue and bone, and determine whether you have underlying gum disease.
2. **Switch to a soft-bristled toothbrush** and review your brushing technique. The goal is to clean the teeth thoroughly without scrubbing the gum margin.
3. **If you have sensitivity**, a desensitising toothpaste used daily may help in the short term, but the underlying cause still needs assessment.
4. **Avoid abrasive whitening products and stiff brushes** until your dentist has reviewed the area.

Depending on what your dentist finds, the visit may include a professional cleaning to remove calculus from the gum margin, a discussion of whether the gum disease is contributing, and advice on whether any further follow-up — including a specialist referral — is appropriate. In most Moderate cases this is not yet a surgical situation.

## What this stage does *not* mean

Moderate recession does not mean you need a gum graft or surgery in most cases. It does not mean you will lose the affected tooth. It does mean that the cause has progressed enough that home-only management is no longer sufficient — the recession should now be assessed and managed in partnership with a dental professional.

# Missing tooth — what your screening identified

Your screening report has flagged one or more teeth as missing. Unlike most other findings on the report, this one does not have severity bands — a tooth is either present or it isn't, so the screening reports it as a single state.

The screening identifies a missing tooth by comparing the photograph of your dentition against the expected positions of the 32 adult teeth. If a position is empty (and not because the tooth is unerupted, as can be the case with wisdom teeth in younger adults), the tooth is flagged as missing.

## What this typically reflects

A missing tooth on your report can have many backgrounds:

- A previous extraction (decayed, infected, or otherwise unrestorable tooth removed in the past)
- A tooth lost to trauma (sports injury, accident, fall)
- A tooth that was removed for orthodontic reasons (to make room for alignment)
- A wisdom tooth that was extracted electively
- A tooth that was congenitally missing — never developed in the first place
- Less commonly, a tooth that has not yet erupted (relevant in younger adults)

The screening cannot distinguish between these histories — it can only confirm the position is empty. You will know best what the cause was for each missing tooth in your case.

## Why missing teeth matter

Each tooth in the mouth has a job — chewing, supporting the bite, holding the position of neighbouring teeth, and contributing to the appearance of the smile. When a tooth is missing and not replaced, several things can happen over months or years:

- **Adjacent teeth drift.** The teeth on either side of the gap can tilt or shift into the empty space.
- **The opposing tooth supererupts.** The tooth on the opposite jaw that used to bite against the missing tooth may grow longer because it no longer has anything to bite against.
- **Bite changes.** As teeth shift, the way the upper and lower jaws meet can change, which over time can put extra stress on the remaining teeth or the jaw joints.
- **Bone resorption.** The bone that used to support the missing tooth gradually shrinks because it is no longer being stimulated by chewing forces. This matters if you ever want to replace the tooth with an implant.
- **Chewing function declines.** Several missing teeth, especially molars, can make it harder to chew certain foods comfortably.
- **Speech and aesthetics.** Front-teeth gaps can affect speech and confidence.

These consequences develop slowly. A single missing molar that has been missing for many years may already have caused the adjacent teeth to drift; replacing it now is more involved than it would have been at the time of loss. This is one reason the screening surfaces missing teeth even if they have been missing for a long time.

## What the Potential Actions table may suggest

If your report includes a Potential Actions entry for a missing tooth, the recommendation is typically one of:

- **Partial Denture** — a removable appliance that fills in one or more missing teeth. Usually the most affordable option and a common starting point for multiple missing teeth.
- **Bridge** — a fixed (non-removable) replacement that uses the adjacent teeth as anchors.
- **Implant** — a fixed replacement that uses a titanium post placed into the jawbone to support a single artificial tooth or a row of teeth.

Each option has different costs, durability, requirements (such as adequate jawbone for an implant), and trade-offs. The chatbot has separate entries for each treatment type.

## What to do next

A missing tooth on its own is not a dental emergency, but it does warrant a planned dental conversation. A reasonable next step is to:

1. **Book a dental appointment** to discuss replacement options. The dentist can assess the bone and adjacent teeth, take any necessary X-rays, and outline which replacement options are suitable in your case.
2. **Check your subsidy and Medisave eligibility** — depending on the procedure (extraction-related work and certain restorations are Medisave-claimable; partial dentures may have CHAS subsidies for eligible card holders). The chatbot can guide you through this.
3. **Consider the time factor.** The longer a tooth has been missing, the more the adjacent teeth and bone may have shifted, and the more involved any replacement becomes. Earlier conversations are easier conversations.

## What this finding does *not* mean

A missing tooth on your screening does not mean replacement is mandatory. Some missing teeth (particularly wisdom teeth, or teeth removed for orthodontic spacing) do not need to be replaced. The dentist will help you decide whether replacement is appropriate, what the trade-offs of each option are, and what the cost and time commitment looks like.

- "Why does my report say I have plaque?"

# Plaque — what your screening identified

Plaque is a soft, sticky film of bacteria that builds up on your teeth throughout the day. It forms naturally — every mouth contains bacteria, and these bacteria stick to the tooth surface and to each other to form plaque. When your screening report flags plaque on a tooth, it means the photos showed visible accumulation that is more than what regular daily brushing should leave behind.

Plaque starts forming within hours of brushing, which is why dentists recommend brushing twice a day and cleaning between teeth daily. Left undisturbed, plaque hardens within a couple of days into a substance called *calculus* (or tartar), which can no longer be removed with a toothbrush and requires professional cleaning.

## What the screening looks for

The screening identifies visible plaque accumulation, which typically appears as:

- A pale yellowish or whitish film along the gum line
- A duller, less reflective tooth surface compared to a freshly cleaned tooth
- Visible deposits in the gaps between teeth or where teeth meet the gum
- Discoloured patches near the gum margin, particularly on the cheek-side surfaces of back teeth

In more advanced cases the screening may also pick up signs of calculus (hardened plaque) appearing as harder, more discoloured deposits at the gum line, or signs of gum inflammation that often accompanies long-standing plaque accumulation.

## Why plaque matters

Plaque is the root cause of the two most common dental conditions: tooth decay (cavities) and gum disease. The bacteria in plaque produce acids when they encounter sugars and starches from food — and those acids erode tooth enamel over time. The same bacteria irritate the gum tissue, causing inflammation, bleeding, and eventually gum recession and bone loss if left unaddressed.

Plaque is the most controllable risk factor in oral health. Unlike many dental concerns, plaque accumulation responds directly to changes you make in your daily oral care routine — which is why the screening flags it as something worth understanding and addressing.

## What typically causes accumulation

Plaque builds up faster than expected when:

- Brushing technique misses certain areas (often the back teeth, the inside surfaces, and the gum line)
- Brushing is too brief (effective brushing takes about two minutes)
- Cleaning between teeth is skipped or inconsistent (a toothbrush cannot reach between teeth)
- Frequent snacking or sugary drinks give the bacteria more fuel
- Saliva flow is reduced (some medications, mouth breathing, or dehydration can contribute)
- Tooth crowding or restorations create areas that are harder to clean

## What to do next

Plaque is not a clinical diagnosis — only a dentist can confirm what you have, what's underneath it, and whether any damage has been done. The severity band on your report (Initial, Moderate, or Advanced) tells you how urgent that review is — see the severity-specific entries for what each band means for you.

# Initial plaque — what your screening identified

Your screening report has flagged Initial-stage plaque accumulation on one or more teeth. This is the earliest band on Oral Link's severity scale and means visible plaque has been picked up in the photos, but the build-up is still mild and there are no obvious signs of complications such as gum inflammation or hardened calculus deposits.

## What this means for you

Initial-stage plaque is the most favourable category to be in. It means the screening has surfaced the early visible signs *before* plaque has had time to harden into calculus or to cause noticeable gum problems. At this stage the situation is almost entirely under your control through changes to your daily oral care routine.

The most likely reasons Initial plaque has been flagged are:

- Brushing technique missing certain areas (commonly the back of the mouth, the inside surfaces of teeth, or right at the gum line)
- Brushing for too short a time (less than the recommended two minutes)
- Skipping or inconsistent cleaning between teeth
- Recent timing of the photos relative to brushing (plaque begins re-forming within hours)

Initial plaque does not usually cause symptoms. There may be some mild gum redness or occasional bleeding when brushing or flossing, but most people don't notice anything different.

## What to do next

Initial-stage plaque is not urgent in the sense that it requires immediate clinical intervention. It is, however, a useful early signal — exactly what screening is designed for so you can act before complications develop.

A reasonable next step is to:

1. **Continue routine dental check-ups** at your normal interval (typically six months). Mention the screening at your next visit so the dentist or dental therapist can show you exactly which areas you are missing.
2. **Review your brushing technique.** The most effective change is usually angling the brush at about 45 degrees toward the gum line and using small circular or gentle back-and-forth motions, focusing on each tooth in turn. Brushing for the full two minutes makes a measurable difference.
3. **Add interdental cleaning if you don't already.** Floss, interdental brushes, or a water flosser — whichever you'll actually use consistently — clears plaque from between teeth where a toothbrush cannot reach.
4. **Consider an electric toothbrush.** Independent reviews consistently show that powered brushes (especially oscillating-rotating and sonic types) remove more plaque than manual brushing for most people, particularly in hard-to-reach areas.

## What this stage does *not* mean

Initial plaque does not mean you have gum disease, cavities, or any clinical problem requiring treatment. It does not mean you need a special dental appointment outside of your routine recall. It is a "tune-up your home routine" signal, not a "see the dentist now" signal.

# Moderate plaque — what your screening identified

Your screening report has flagged Moderate-stage plaque accumulation on one or more teeth. At this stage the build-up is no longer mild — the plaque has been present long enough to be clearly visible in the photographs, and there may be early signs of complications such as gum inflammation or hardened deposits beginning to form.

## What this means for you

Moderate-stage plaque typically means home oral hygiene has not been keeping up with the bacterial build-up over an extended period. You may not have noticed anything obvious because plaque-related problems often develop without pain in the early stages, but the visible evidence is now meaningful enough that the screening has surfaced it.

Common features at this stage include:

- Plaque visible along the gum line on multiple teeth, particularly on the cheek-side surfaces of back teeth and the tongue-side surfaces of the lower front teeth
- Possible early calculus (tartar) deposits, especially behind the lower front teeth where saliva pools
- Gums that appear redder than normal, may bleed when brushing or flossing, and feel tender in places (this is *gingivitis* — the early reversible stage of gum disease)
- A persistent feeling that teeth are not as smooth as they should be after brushing

## What typically drives it

Moderate plaque rarely arises from one missed brushing — it reflects a sustained gap between bacterial accumulation and what your routine is removing. Common contributing factors:

- Brushing technique that consistently misses the same areas
- No regular interdental cleaning, or inconsistent flossing
- Long gaps between professional dental cleanings (calculus, once formed, requires a dentist or dental hygienist to remove)
- A diet with frequent sugar or starch exposures (snacking, sugary drinks throughout the day)
- Crowded or rotated teeth that create hard-to-clean areas
- Reduced saliva flow due to medication or other factors

## What to do next

Moderate plaque accumulation calls for a planned dental visit, not a routine recall:

1. **Book a professional cleaning within the next four to six weeks.** A dentist or dental hygienist can remove the calculus and disrupted plaque that you cannot reach with home tools, and identify any areas where gum inflammation has started.
2. **Use the visit to get personalised technique advice.** Ask the dentist or therapist to point out the specific teeth and surfaces where you are missing — this is far more useful than generic brushing tips.
3. **Upgrade your home routine.** If you don't already use interdental cleaning, start; if you brush manually, an electric toothbrush often makes a marked difference. Two minutes of effective brushing twice a day is the minimum target.
4. **Reduce snacking frequency.** It's not just total sugar that drives plaque growth, but how often the bacteria get fed. Stretching gaps between meals and avoiding sipping sugary drinks across the day helps.
5. **Watch for bleeding gums.** Bleeding when brushing or flossing is a sign of gingivitis. With consistent home care plus the professional cleaning, gingivitis is reversible — usually within two to three weeks.

## What this stage does *not* mean

Moderate plaque does not mean you have advanced gum disease, periodontitis, or that you have caused permanent damage. It does not mean you need any specialist referral in most cases. It does mean that home care alone is no longer enough — a professional cleaning combined with adjustments to your routine is the right intervention.

# Advanced plaque — what your screening identified

Your screening report has flagged Advanced-stage plaque accumulation on one or more teeth. At this stage the build-up is heavy and well established, calculus (hardened plaque) is almost certainly present, and there is a meaningful likelihood that gum inflammation or early periodontal disease has developed alongside it.

## What this means for you

Advanced plaque means the bacterial build-up has been progressing without effective intervention for a long enough period that home care can no longer reverse it on its own. Even perfect brushing from today onwards cannot remove existing calculus — only a professional cleaning (scaling) can, and the surrounding gum tissue may already need attention.

Common features at this stage include:

- Heavy visible plaque and calculus along the gum line on multiple teeth
- Calculus that may be visible above the gum line as a discoloured (yellowish, brownish, or greyish) hard deposit, and likely also below the gum line where it cannot be seen but contributes to gum disease
- Red, swollen gums that bleed readily when brushed or flossed
- Possible gum recession, exposed root surfaces, or gum tenderness
- Bad breath that does not resolve with normal brushing, or a persistent unpleasant taste
- In some cases, loose teeth or a feeling that the bite has changed — this is a sign that periodontal disease may be affecting the bone supporting the teeth

## What this often signals

Advanced plaque rarely exists in isolation. It usually coexists with at least gingivitis (gum inflammation) and may be part of an active periodontitis — chronic gum disease in which the bone and ligament supporting the teeth break down. Other factors that often appear together with Advanced plaque:

- Long-standing absence of professional dental cleanings
- Smoking, which markedly accelerates gum disease
- Poorly controlled diabetes, which has a two-way relationship with gum disease
- Crowded teeth or restorations that create areas where plaque accumulates undisturbed

## What to do next

Advanced plaque accumulation warrants prompt dental review:

1. **Book a dental appointment within the next one to two weeks.** This is no longer a routine timeline. The dentist needs to assess the gum tissue, measure the depth of any gum pockets that have formed, and identify whether scaling alone is sufficient or whether deeper periodontal treatment is needed.
2. **Expect more than a single cleaning visit.** A typical course of treatment for Advanced plaque-related gum disease may involve scaling and root planing (a deeper professional cleaning under the gum line), often spread across two visits, and a follow-up review four to six weeks later to assess gum healing.
3. **Be prepared for the possibility of a periodontist referral.** If the screening and clinical examination together suggest the gum disease has progressed beyond what a general dentist normally manages, you may be referred to a periodontist — a specialist in gum and supporting-tissue conditions.
4. **Maintain very gentle but thorough oral hygiene in the meantime.** Use a soft-bristled brush, brush twice a day, clean between teeth daily, and don't be alarmed if the gums bleed initially — bleeding usually decreases as the gum tissue becomes healthier.
5. **Address contributing factors.** If you smoke, this is a high-impact moment to discuss stopping with your dentist or doctor. If you have diabetes, ensure it is well controlled — gum disease and blood sugar control are closely linked.

## In an emergency

If you experience severe pain, swelling that affects breathing or swallowing, or a sudden marked increase in tooth mobility, do not wait for a routine appointment. Seek emergency dental or medical care.

## What this stage does *not* mean

Advanced plaque does not automatically mean you will lose teeth. With timely periodontal care many advanced cases can be stabilised, even when the gum disease has progressed. It does mean that home management alone is no longer adequate and that professional intervention — and likely ongoing periodontal maintenance — is the right path forward.
